**What Does a Data Analyst Do?**

- Collects, organizes, and interprets data to help companies make informed decisions.
- Turns raw data into actionable insights!!
- Starts with asking the right business questions.
- Gathers data, cleans data, analyzes data, presents findings in dashboards to clearly communicate with stakeholders.
- Retrieves, gathers, interprets data — includes identifying patterns and transforming interpreted data into insightful reports for teams/clients.
- Tools: Excel/spreadsheets, Tableau, SQL, Python, possibly R.

| Tool              | Purpose                                                                                                                                         |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| SQL               | Answers questions like "who signed up in the last 15 days and made a purchase?"                                                                 |
| Excel             | For ad hoc analysis, pivot tables, quick calculations                                                                                           |
| Tableau, Power BI | Easy-to-understand visuals                                                                                                                      |
| Basic Stats       | E.g., if a company sees a revenue increase, statistics can help determine whether it's a seasonal trend, marketing efforts, or random variation |

**Different types of analysts:**

- **Business Intelligence analyst** — builds dashboards, tracks key performance indicators, relies on SQL/Tableau/Power BI, influences high-level business strategies through data.
- **Marketing analyst** — focuses on customer behavior, advertising performance, and campaign effectiveness; aims for higher ROI. Gathers data from surveys, Google Analytics, Meta Ads, and SQL to track/report digital campaign status.
- **Product analyst** — analyzes user behavior, feature adoption, retention rates, and A/B testing to inform product decisions. Uses SQL and dashboard tools to track engagement and recommend ways to improve product performance.
- **Operations analyst** — focuses on improving efficiency of internal business processes: supply chains, workflows, resource allocation, process bottlenecks. Streamlines operations with Excel, SQL, Python, and maybe R.

**Descriptive Analytics: What It Is and Related Terms** 

- process of parsing historical data to better understand the changes that occur in a business
- using a range of historical data and benchmarking
- examples of metrics used in descriptive analytics include year-over-year pricing changes, month-over-month sales growth, the number of users, or the total revenue per subscriber
- used with newer analytics like predictive and prescriptive analytics
- two primary methods by which data is collected for descriptive analytics. These are data aggregation and data mining
- return on invested capital (ROIC) is a form of descriptive analytics created by taking three data points—net income, dividends, and total capital

- a report showing sales of $1 million may sound impressive, but it lacks context. If that figure represents a 20% month-over-month decline, there is cause for concern. If it is a 40% YOY increase, then it suggests something is going right with the sales or marketing strategy

**What Is Diagnostic Analytics? A Quick Guide**

- diagnostic analytics examines data to understand why events, behaviors, and outcomes happened
- helps teams identify root causes, discover patterns, and explain relationships 
- fills in the gap between descriptive and predictive to give needed context and details to the data, helping you make more precise choices by fully understanding influencing factors 
- iterative process; as you uncover insights you can refine your hypotheses, perform additional analyses, or delve deeper into the data to gain a better understanding of it

**Helps answer critical business questions like:** 
1. Why did sales drop last quarter?
2. What factors influenced customer churn?
3. Which marketing campaigns drove the highest conversions?
4. How did product changes affect user engagement?
5. What caused the spike in support tickets?

**key purposes of diagnostic analytics:**
Problem solving: 
- root cause identification, identify what drives problems or successes 
- issue prevention: stop problems before they happen again 

Performance improvement:
- process optimization: spot bottlenecks and inefficiencies
- strategy evaluation: see what's working and what isn't

Data validation: 
- hypothesis testing: confirm or challenge your assumptions
- quality assurance: identify data inconsistencies and errors 

Risk management: 
- outcome prediction: understand the likelihood of specific results
- mitigation planning: develop strategies to reduce risks

**Most common diagnostic analytics techniques:**
- Hypothesis testing: Form a hypothesis about relationships between variables and use statistical tests to support or reject it
- Regression analysis: Understand how one or more independent variables affect a dependent variable.
- Anomaly detection: Find unusual patterns that may indicate underlying issues
- Root cause analysis: Investigate contributing factors to identify the primary cause behind an outcome
- Correlation analysis: Measure the strength and direction of relationships between variables
- Cohort analysis: Compare groups with shared traits over time to see different responses and outcomes
- Factor analysis: Reduce data complexity by identifying underlying factors that explain relationships
- Case-control studies: Compare cases and controls to find factors associated with an outcome
- Time series analysis: Examine data over consistent time intervals to find trends and seasonality
- Simulation modeling: Use computational simulations to see how variable changes affect outcomes
- Data visualization: Communicate complex relationships clearly through charts and graphs

**What is predictive analytics?**
- predictive analytics is an advanced branch of data science that uses historical data, statistical modeling, and machine learning to answer the question, “What might happen next?"
- using data to forecast future outcomes with a high degree of precision
- identify patterns in historic or current data to predict behaviors

For all analysis types, the order of operations is like this:
1. define the problem
2. acquire and organize the data
3. pre-process the data/data cleaning 
4. develop predictive models (regression, decision trees)
5. validate and deploy results 

**Predictive analytics techniques**
- Regression analysis: Estimates relationships between variables to determine how one input (like price) affects another (like sales)
- Decision trees: Classification models that map out potential choices and their results in a tree-like structure
- Neural networks: Powerhouse pattern recognition engines that model complex, nonlinear relationships in data

**Understanding Prescriptive Analytics: Process, Benefits, and Applications**

- prescriptive analytics uses AI and machine learning to recommend strategies for businesses, enhancing decision making with minimal human intervention
- it works with predictive analytics to suggest optimal actions by modeling future outcomes based on current and historical data
- this type of analytics helps prevent fraud and improve efficiency but requires accurate data inputs to be effective
- prescriptive analytics is used in sectors like healthcare and financial services to manage risk and enhance performance
- it is best suited for short-term solutions, as its reliability decreases over longer time frames

simply put: it seeks to understand what is needed to achieve an aim

**Prescriptive analytics could be used to:**

In General:
- Evaluate whether a local fire department should require residents to evacuate a particular area when a wildfire is burning nearby
- Predict whether an article on a particular topic will be popular with readers based on data about searches and social shares for related topics
- Adjust a worker training program in real time based on how the worker is responding to each lesson

In Healthcare:
- be used to analyze which hospital patients have the highest risk of readmission so that healthcare providers can do more

In Operations:
- can help maximize your company’s profits by automatically adjusting ticket prices and availability based on numerous factors, including customer demand, weather, and gasoline prices
- When the algorithm identifies that this year’s pre-Christmas ticket sales from Los Angeles to New York are behind last year’s, for example, it can automatically lower prices, while making sure not to drop them too low in light of this year’s higher oil prices.

In Banking:
- Improve ways to cross-sell and upsell products and services
- Develop key security and regulatory initiatives, like compliance reporting

In Marketing:
- stay ahead of consumer trends by using past trends and past performance to give internal and external marketing departments a competitive edge
- advertising for a certain demographic during the Super Bowl

**Bottom line**
Prescriptive analytics analyzes raw data about past trends and performance through machine learning (meaning very little, if any, human input) to determine possible courses of action or new strategies, generally for the near term. **Prescriptive analytics uses past data and predictions to recommend future actions.**

**Analysis/Reporting with Excel**
**If Function**
- performs a logical test and returns one result when the logical test returns TRUE and another when the logical test returns FALSE
- EXAMPLE: to "pass" scores above 70: =IF(A1>70,"Pass","Fail")

SYNTAX: =IF(logical_test, [value_if_true], [value_if_false])
^logical_test is a value/expression that is T/F, args are optional

- =IF(B5>80,TRUE) makes results flip to t/f
- =IF(B5>80,"x") makes results flip to x/f
- =IF(B5>80,"x","") make results flip to x/""
- =IF(C5<70,"Fail","Pass") makes results flip to fail/pass

| Goal | Logical test | 
| If A1 is greater than 75 | A1>75 |
| If A1 equals 100 | A1=100 |
| If A1 is less than or equal to 100 | A1 <= 100 |
| If A1 equals "Red" | A1 = "red" |
| If A1 is not equal to "Red" | A1 <> "red" |
| If A1 is less than B1 | A1 < B1 |
| If A1 is empty | A1 = "" |
| If A1 is not empty | A1 <> "" |
| If A1 is less than the current date | A1 < TODAY() |

- most widely used functions in Excel
- Works with all comparison operators (=, <>, >, <, >=, <=)
- Both value_if_true and value_if_false are optional, but at least one must be provided
- Can be combined with AND, OR, and NOT for complex logical tests
- Supports nested IF statements for multiple conditions ("else if" logic)
- Can be combined with SEARCH and ISNUMBER for simple pattern matching
- Can be combined with REGEXTEST for advanced pattern matching
- Can return empty strings ("") to make cells appear blank

more examples: 
- =IF(OR(B5="red",B5="green"),"x","") <- if color is red or green return x, if this is false leave blank
- =IF(AND(C5 = "B", D5 >=80), "x", "") <- if c5 is b and d5 greater than 80, return x, else return blank
- =IF(AND(OR(C5 = "A", C5 = "B"), D5 >= 80), "x", "") <- c5 can either be a or b AND d5 has to be greater than or equal to 80 for it to return x (aka true), blank if false

**Nestled Ifs**
moooore examples: 
- =IF(B5="red",100,IF(B5="blue",125,IF(B5="green",150))) <- basically if and elifs and else; if color is red return 100 (aka true), else if the color is blue return 125 (aka true), else if the color is green return 150 (aka true)
- =IF(B5="red",100,B5="blue",125,B5="green",150) ^same as above but without the nestled ifs because ifs function can handle multiple logical tests without nesting

**Switch functions**
- =SWITCH(B5,"red",100,"blue",125,"green",150) <- does the same thing as the nestled if function but even simplier!!!!

**Return another formula with IF**
- =IF(B5="red",D5*1.2,D5) <- if the color is red, increase the price by 20%, otherwise return to the price listed in the D5 column

**Root Cause Analysis**
**Use the five whys!**

- One very common question is, “What is the root cause of the problem?”... by identifying and eliminating the root cause, data professionals can help stop that problem from occurring again.  

solution: The five whys is a simple but effective technique for identifying a root cause. It involves asking "Why?" repeatedly until the answer reveals itself.

**examples**
- An online grocery store was receiving numerous customer service complaints about poor deliveries. To address this problem, a data analyst at the company asked their first “why?”

1. “Customers are complaining about poor grocery deliveries. Why?”
2. “Products are arriving damaged. Why?”
3. “Products are not packaged properly. Why?”
4. “Grocery packers are not adequately trained. Why?”
5. “Packers have not completed required training. Why?”

Solution: in this example, the root cause of the problem was that HR had not completed the training program updates and was using a less-thorough guide to train new packers. Fortunately, this was a problem that the grocer could control. 

Next example: 

An irrigation company was experiencing an increase in the number of defects in their water pumps. The company's data team used the five whys to analyze the situation:

1. “There has been an increase in the number of defects in water pumps. Why?”
2. “The machines are not properly calibrated. Why?”
3. “The machines were miscalibrated during maintenance. Why?”
4. “The calibration method is inappropriate for the machines. Why?”
5. “The engineers don’t have the information they need to calibrate the upgraded machines. Why?”

Solution: the root cause of the problem was that the engineers lacked important information about how to calibrate the machines using the new software system. The solution was found, and the irrigation company was able to implement it right away.

- eg. age is considered continuous even though it is given in years 
- categorical is divided into nominal and ordinal 
- nominal = there is no order associated with the values 
- eg. the color of your eyes
- ordinal = some kind of order associated with the values 
- for a categorical var to be ordinal, you must be able to assign an "algebric" order
- eg. freshman, sophmore, junior, senior; ranking makes sense 
- months of the year? this would be nominal
